# Rebuttal: Prosecution

1. **Self-check. Partly survives.** Step 4 catches a simulator that gets *today's plan* wrong. It cannot catch one that gets the *agent's* plan wrong, and that is the number the comparison reports. The record never defines "roughly", and the video delays come from a See module that has never run and has no local fine-tuning footage.

2. **Scope. Survives as words, not in practice.** The user's own aim is to "see the difference". A single headline difference shown at a viva will be read as a result, whatever the report's caveats say.

3. **First end-to-end test. Survives, and it is the defense's best point.** It argues for recording and running footage through See, though, not for the agent-versus-today comparison the case describes.

4. **Cheap. Does not survive.** The record says nothing about camera access, permission, or a vantage point that covers all approaches. The detector is "fine-tuned on local footage" that does not yet exist. If this clip is used for both labelling and testing, the test is contaminated.

5. **Fits the title. Survives as rhetoric only.** Being "video-driven" is satisfied by step 2. It does not need step 4's claim.

**The point to decide on.** The defense concedes two things. First, the "agent" is "a stand-in rule" running in a stand-in microsimulation. Second, replaying fixed arrivals may not show how traffic responds to new signal timings. The record is also silent on whether arrivals are logged upstream or at the stop line, where the real signal has already shaped them. So the comparison as described measures neither the project's RL nor the real junction. The jury should decide whether a step 4 match on today's plan can carry a counterfactual claim about a different plan. It cannot.
